# Do binding court rules on AI say anything about what lawyers put into AI? A pre-registered check

Pre-registered 30 October 2026, before the texts named under "Test" were read. By MV2 of Munim, Inc. (mv2@munimtech.com); this analysis was made with AI agents working in his name. Text CC BY 4.0. It describes public documents. It is not legal advice.

## The question

Lawyers' duties around generative AI fall on two sides:

- **Output**: checking what the tool produces (citations, quotations, facts) before it is filed.
- **Input**: deciding what client or court information may be typed or uploaded into the tool at all (confidentiality, sealed records, retention, training on inputs).

Public trackers already note that the statewide court rules of 2026 require *verification, not disclosure* of AI use (for example legalaigovernance.com, "Statewide AI Court Rules in 2026: Verification, Not Disclosure", 1 October 2026). That article does not discuss the input side. This check asks the next question: **does any binding court rule tell lawyers what they may put into an AI tool?**

## What the index shows before the test (bar-ai-guidance 0.11.0, 49 entries)

Six entries are binding (`binding` is `court_rule` or `statute`). Read from each entry's `confidentiality_note`, which was written from the primary text:

| Entry | Binding | Output side | Input side |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ca-sb-574` (California statute, from 1 Jan 2027) | statute | every citation checked | **yes**: no confidential, personal identifying or other nonpublic information into a generative AI system unless access is limited to the attorney and people bound to keep it confidential |
| `co-rule-change-2026-02` (Colorado, Rule 1.1 comment and Scope) | court rule | general | pointer only: lists Rule 1.6 among the rules AI use can implicate; no new duty |
| `fl-sc2026-0673-rule-2-515` (Florida Rule 2.515) | court rule | cited authorities exist and are accurate | none |
| `in-trial-rule-11-2026` (Indiana Trial Rule 11) | court rule | cited authority is authentic | none |
| `ny-ucs-part-161` (New York Part 161) | court rule | review for fabricated material | none in the notice read so far |
| `ri-rule-1-1-2026` (Rhode Island Rule 1.1 comment) | court rule | competence about technology | none in the order; its advisory interim guidelines cover input |

Where courts do speak to input, the instruments in the index are advisory for lawyers (some bind court staff): Arkansas Administrative Order No. 25 as published for comment (find out whether a system keeps and uses confidential or sealed data), the Illinois Supreme Court policy, North Dakota's 2026 public notice, South Carolina's interim policy, Louisiana's and Hawaiʻi's letters, New Jersey's preliminary guidelines and Rhode Island's interim guidelines.

## Claim to be tested

Among court rules that bind lawyers' use of generative AI at state level, none governs what a lawyer may put into an AI tool; they govern what comes out (verification of citations and authorities) or restate competence. The only binding input rule in the index is California's statute SB 574, which starts on 1 January 2027. Input duties otherwise come from the confidentiality rule (Rule 1.6) as read by bar opinions and from advisory court guidance.

## Test (texts not yet read when this was written)

1. **Connecticut Practice Book Section 4-9** ("Generative AI Compliance", effective 23 June 2026) and the revised Section 4-2(b), read from the Connecticut Law Journal of 23 June 2026. Prediction: verification and certification only; nothing on confidential information entering AI.
2. **New York Part 161** (22 NYCRR), read from the court system's own text if it can be fetched. Prediction: nothing binding on input.
3. **Texas Misc. Docket No. 26-9054** (preliminary approval, 26 June 2026, not in force as of 2 October 2026), read from txcourts.gov. Prediction: verification only. It counts only as a proposal.
4. **Arkansas Administrative Order No. 25 as adopted** (December 2025, per trackers), if its adopted text can be read. Prediction: Section 1 stays advisory awareness language for lawyers; the binding prohibition in Section 2 covers court staff and others with access to internal court data, not lawyers' own client data.

**The claim fails** if any of these texts, read in full, imposes a binding duty on lawyers about what may be entered into an AI tool. Each result will be reported as found, including any that go against the prediction.

## Limits

- State-level instruments only. Hundreds of federal and local standing orders exist and are not covered; some of them address confidentiality.
- The index covers 49 documents from the ABA, 33 states and DC; a state with no entry may still have a rule.
- "Binding" follows the index's own `binding` field. Section 2 of Arkansas's order and South Carolina's policy bind court staff; that is noted but not counted as a rule on lawyers.
- A rule that says nothing about input does not leave lawyers free: Rule 1.6 and the bar opinions still apply.
