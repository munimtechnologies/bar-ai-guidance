# bar-ai-guidance

A small, verified index of what US bars, courts and legislatures have told lawyers about using generative AI, with a **primary-source link for every row**. Since 0.6.0 it also records **what self-hosted AI tools let people reach out of the box**, the software side of the same confidentiality question. Since 0.14.0 it also lists court policies on judges' and court staff's own AI use that say which AI systems may receive non-public court information, because their definitions are the closest thing to a court-made test of a safe system; each such entry says that it does not govern lawyers. Since November 2026 a separate file lists the **AI rules of US administrative tribunals** (agency judges and boards, not courts), which court trackers mostly leave out. JSON files and tests, no dependencies.

Made by MV2 of Munim, Inc., with AI agents working in his name. It is information about public documents and public source code, not legal advice or an ethics opinion.

## Why

By October 2026 more than twenty-five US jurisdictions had issued opinions, guidance, rule changes or, in California's case, a statute on lawyers' use of generative AI, and the public hallucination-case database kept by Damien Charlotin listed over 2,000 decisions. Law firm trackers summarise the guidance behind HTML pages or paywalls. Nothing small and machine-readable pointed at the **issuing bar's, court's or legislature's own page or PDF** for each document, with a record of when the link was last checked. That is what this is.

## What is in it

`data/guidance.json` holds one entry per document:

| field | meaning |
|---|---|
| `jurisdiction` | `US` for the ABA, otherwise the state code or `DC` |
| `issuer`, `title`, `number`, `date` | as the document itself states them (`YYYY-MM` when it gives no day, `YYYY` when only the year is known, with `notes` saying how) |
| `kind` | `formal_opinion`, `advisory_opinion`, `guidance`, `court_notice`, `court_policy`, `rule_amendment` or `statute` |
| `binding` | `advisory`; `court_rule` for an amendment a court adopted into its rules; `statute` for an enacted law |
| `url` | the issuer's own page or PDF, never an aggregator; the one exception is an `archived` entry (below) |
| `url_verified`, `verification` | when MV2 last checked the link, and whether the page was `fetched` and its title matched, only `cited` (the issuer's own address, not retrieved; `notes` then say why and where the details came from), or `archived` (the issuer no longer serves the document at its own address, so the link is an Internet Archive capture of the issuer's own file, which was retrieved and read; `notes` say when and why) |
| `confidentiality_note` | a one-sentence paraphrase of the confidentiality point, in MV2's words |
| `in_house_tools_note` | what the document says about tools run inside the firm, if anything |

Version 0.14.0 added California's Rule of Court 10.430, Delaware's and Pennsylvania's interim policies for judicial officers and court personnel, and Oklahoma's 2026 order on generative AI, each read in the issuer's own page or PDF. The three court policies draw the line for non-public court information in three different ways: California by who can reach or train on the data (a system the court creates or manages is not public), Pennsylvania by what the system does with entered data (a Secured AI System retains, transfers and exposes nothing), and Delaware by whether the court's Administrative Office has approved the tool. Version 0.15.0 added Vermont's Administrative Directive No. 38 as amended on 6 October 2025, Iowa's Chapter 27 rules and the DC Courts' internal AI use policy, each read in the issuer's own PDF. Vermont's directive is the first state-court instrument in the index that puts a duty about what goes into AI on everyone who uses the courts, lawyers included; it is an administrative directive, not a rule of procedure, and it does not define a safe system. A sandboxed reading of the amendment's underline and strike-through markup showed that this sentence was neither added nor deleted in 2025, so it is in force. Iowa's rules require committee approval of any AI tool, in-house or vendor, before judges or their assistants use it with case information, and the DC policy keeps court information out of tools whose provider does not promise to keep it confidential. Where MV2's tool could not extract a PDF's text, the entry's `notes` say so and say where the date and paraphrase came from; where the text was read, the notes say that too.

Version 0.16.0 added two documents and a new verification value:

The bar's former public file address (msbar.ctsites.net) no longer resolves, and its ethics opinions page now sends readers to a member-login research library, so the entry links an Internet Archive capture (10 July 2026) of the bar's own PDF, which MV2 read in full. - **New York State Bar Association, Legal Ethics Guidelines for Lawyers' Use of Artificial Intelligence**, adopted by the House of Delegates on 31 October 2026 (106-page PDF, version 7 of 23 October 2026, with comments). Version 0.16.1 replaced the entry's announcement-based paraphrase with one read from the report itself: Guidelines 6A and 6B ask a lawyer to understand and control where client information goes (to an outside service, into retention or training, or across matters) before using a tool, and the report's definition of an AI System makes a lawyer responsible for the design and configuration choices in AI the firm builds or sets up itself.
- The Pennsylvania Bar Association's 2024 opinion now notes that its pabar.org address returned 404 on 6 November 2026 (an archived copy exists; the path sits under /Members/).

Version 0.16.2 added Connecticut's amendments to its Rules of Appellate Procedure (Sections 60-4, 62-6 (d) and 85-2, in force since 14 July 2026), which carry the Superior Court's Section 4-9 duty to verify AI-produced citations into appeals and make failing to do so a ground for sanctions (`ct-rap-ai-2026`).

## AI rules of US administrative tribunals (`data/agency_ai_rules.json`, 0.4.0: seventeen instruments)

Court trackers follow the AI rules courts set for filers; agency adjudicators (workers' compensation boards, tax and labor tribunals, offices of administrative hearings, immigration courts) mostly fall outside them, although many people appear before them without a lawyer. `data/agency_ai_rules.json` is a first open list: 17 instruments (7 federal, 10 state), 12 of them not in the largest court-order tracker. Each entry gives the tribunal, the instrument, its date and status, whether it asks for disclosure or certification of AI use, whether it tells filers to check AI output, the authority it cites, its sanctions, the issuer's own link and how far the entry has been checked (`source_check`). It shows a workers'-compensation cluster (Illinois, South Carolina, Georgia), a public-sector labor-board cluster (California's PERB, Massachusetts's CERB), rules made by deciding a case (PERB's *Atwater*, OCAHO's *Wallcon*), one outright ban on AI use during hearings (Illinois's Workers' Compensation Commission), an Idaho AI rule its legislature rejected, and federal pages that now say "Super Intelligence" after Executive Order 14434. It is a convenience list, not a census: [write-up](analysis/agency-ai-rules.md).

## Who can reach a self-hosted AI tool out of the box (0.8.0: fifteen tools)

California's **SB 574** (in force 1 January 2027) asks whether access to what an attorney enters into a generative AI system is restricted to the attorney and authorised persons. For a firm that runs its own AI, the answer depends on software settings. `data/self_hosted_defaults.json` records them as shipped for fifteen tools: ten model servers (Ollama, llama.cpp, SGLang, vLLM, LocalAI, KoboldCpp, LMDeploy, LiteLLM Proxy, TabbyAPI, Xinference) and five chat apps (AnythingLLM, text-generation-webui, LibreChat, Dify, Open WebUI). For each it gives the address the tool listens on when started directly and in the project's own container, whether it asks for a credential, whether anyone can sign up, and the admin and sharing defaults. Each fact links the source file or doc page it was read from, and each entry says what was not checked. 0.8.0 added KoboldCpp, LMDeploy, TabbyAPI and Xinference, and dates LiteLLM's master-key check to its first release.

What it shows ([full write-up](analysis/sb574-self-hosted-defaults.md)):

- Seven of the ten model servers ask for no credential by default. vLLM, LocalAI, KoboldCpp and LMDeploy listen on all interfaces even when started directly (KoboldCpp while printing a localhost address). Ollama, llama.cpp and SGLang stay on the machine when started directly, but their own container or compose setups listen on all interfaces.
- The three that demand a credential do it three different ways: LiteLLM's proxy refuses to start without a real master key; TabbyAPI generates random keys at first start; Xinference requires a login, but whoever reaches its setup page first creates the admin. LocalAI refuses an unauthenticated start only on public addresses.
- LiteLLM's check is new: committed 19 September 2026 and first released in v1.104.0 on 3 October 2026, so proxies installed from earlier releases do not have it.
- AnythingLLM's Docker install and text-generation-webui's container have no login until one is turned on. LibreChat lets anyone who reaches it register. Dify is invitation-only once set up, but until the first admin exists whoever opens `/install` first owns it. Open WebUI holds new sign-ups for admin approval, but lets every admin read every user's chat and shows a button that uploads a chat to an outside site.
- In ten of the fifteen, someone never given an account can, as shipped, either use the model or create an account and use the app; Dify and Xinference allow it only before setup. Self-hosting does not restrict access by itself; changed settings do.

This is a record of defaults on 14 October 2026, not a view on whether any office meets SB 574.

## Known gaps in the guidance index

- **MD** (Maryland State Bar Association AI Task Force, *An Overview of Ethical Considerations for Attorney Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence Technologies*, April 2025, 7 pages): sold in the MSBA store at $29 for non-members, free to members; no public copy found.
- **CA rule amendments**: proposed AI amendments to Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.6, 3.3, 5.1 and 5.3 finished public comment on 6 August 2026; no Supreme Court order adopting them found as of 14 October 2026.
- **TX**: Misc. Docket No. 26-9054 (26 June 2026) preliminarily approved amendments to Civil Procedure Rule 13 and Appellate Rule 9.1 under which a signer represents having verified the filing's accuracy, including that citations, quotations and holdings are not fabricated. The Court expected them to take effect on 1 October 2026, but as of 6 November 2026 txcourts.gov lists no final order and the current Rules of Civil Procedure still print the old Rule 13, so this stays a gap until a final order appears.
- The ABA's January 2026 law-firm guidance (americanbar.org refuses automated fetches), and KS, NE, TN and UT (trackers report no formal opinion as of mid-2026; Utah's Judicial Council interim rules on generative AI, in effect from 27 October 2025, are not listed because the rules file asks for a sign-in; trackers say the same of IN, MA and WI, whose Supreme Court rule amendment, Office of Bar Counsel article and State Bar guidance are now listed above).

## Use

```bash
python -m unittest test_bar_ai_guidance test_self_hosted_defaults test_agency_ai_rules test_court_ai_rules_skill test_ai_protective_orders_skill
python -c "import json; d=json.load(open('data/guidance.json')); print(len(d['entries']))"
```

`test_bar_ai_guidance` checks that every URL is HTTPS on a listed primary host, that dates and codes are well formed, that a year-only date always has a note, that rule amendments are marked as court rules and statutes as statutes, that unretrieved links carry notes, that an Internet Archive link appears only on an `archived` entry that wraps a listed former issuer address and says so in its notes, that entries are sorted by jurisdiction, and that notes are paraphrases rather than quotations. `test_self_hosted_defaults` checks the defaults file's fields and enums, that every source is the project's own repository or documentation, that a tool marked as requiring a key says how it avoids a keyless start (refusing to start, or generating a key), that a project recorded without a release says it has none, and that the file draws no legal conclusions. `test_agency_ai_rules` checks the agency file's keys, enums (each documented in the file), dates, that every link is HTTPS on a listed issuer host, and known entries. `test_court_ai_rules_skill` checks the skill's frontmatter, that every command its `SKILL.md` documents runs and every entry id it cites exists, and the script's filters, labels and exit codes on the real index and on a synthetic one, without network access. `test_ai_protective_orders_skill` does the same for the protective-order skill: frontmatter, documented commands and cited case ids, every clause-test code documented, and filters, counts, labels and exit codes on the real dataset and a synthetic one.

## Agent skill: court-ai-rules

`skills/court-ai-rules/` is an agent skill (a `SKILL.md` and a standard-library script) for coding agents such as Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. When an agent helps draft or check a US court filing with AI, or is asked which AI rules apply to lawyers in a state, the skill has it look the state up in `data/guidance.json`, put court rules and statutes before advisory guidance, link the issuer's own text, say plainly when a state has no entry (which does not mean the state has no rules), and work through a pre-filing checklist: state filing rules, pending rules, the court's local rules and the judge's standing orders (not indexed here), disclosure, every citation checked in a primary source, and confidentiality. It is information about public documents, not legal advice.

```bash
npx skills add munimtechnologies/bar-ai-guidance --skill court-ai-rules
python skills/court-ai-rules/scripts/court_ai_rules.py --state CT
python skills/court-ai-rules/scripts/court_ai_rules.py --binding court_rule --json
```

## Agent skill: ai-protective-orders

`skills/ai-protective-orders/` is a second agent skill. Before discovery or disclosure material covered by a protective order goes into an AI tool, or when an AI clause for a stipulated protective order is being drafted, it has the agent look up `data/ai_protective_orders.json`: the AI clauses of 44 US federal protective and confidentiality orders (2025-2026), each with the order's own words, the tests it uses (no training on inputs, no public or consumer tools, a contract with the AI provider, notice to the other side, an outright ban) and whether its literal words allow an openly published model run on the party's own server. The skill starts from the order in the user's own case, quotes words rather than labels, and never says a use complies. It is information about public court orders, not legal advice.

```bash
npx skills add munimtechnologies/bar-ai-guidance --skill ai-protective-orders
python skills/ai-protective-orders/scripts/ai_protective_orders.py --court SDNY --setup permitted
python skills/ai-protective-orders/scripts/ai_protective_orders.py --test CONTRACT --quotes
```

## Corrections

If a link has moved, a date is wrong, a default has changed or a jurisdiction is missing, open an issue with the issuer's or project's URL. MV2 re-checks every link when he changes the file and records the date in `url_verified` or `checked`.
